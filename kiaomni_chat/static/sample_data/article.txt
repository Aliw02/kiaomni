SpaceX Starship: The 2024–2025 Test Campaign and the Path to Reusability

Between March 2024 and January 2025, SpaceX conducted five flight tests of its next-generation launch system, Starship, from its Starbase facility in Boca Chica, Texas. In that ten-month window, the company moved from routine ship losses to catching both the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage on the same launch tower — a feat that has no precedent in the history of spaceflight. This article traces the engineering decisions, the failures, and the small handful of breakthrough successes that redefined what orbital-class reuse is, and explains why the SpaceX test campaign is being studied by every other launch provider in the world as the new template for iterative rocket development.

**The Vehicle**

Starship is the most powerful operational launch system ever built. The fully-stacked vehicle stands 121 meters tall, with the Super Heavy first-stage booster accounting for the bottom 71 meters and the Starship upper stage (sometimes called "Ship") accounting for the top 50. Fully fueled, the vehicle weighs about 5,000 metric tons. The first stage is powered by 33 Raptor 2 engines, each producing about 230 metric tons of thrust at sea level, for a total liftoff thrust of approximately 7,500 metric tons — more than the Saturn V that carried the Apollo astronauts. The upper stage is powered by 6 Raptor engines (3 sea-level Raptor 2, 3 vacuum-optimized Raptor 2). The whole vehicle is designed to be rapidly reusable: the goal is a turnaround time of under 24 hours between flights, with both stages returning to the launch site for catch and refurbishment.

The catch mechanism on the launch tower is the engineering centerpiece. Rather than landing on legs like the Falcon 9 first stage, the Super Heavy booster is designed to be caught by the launch tower itself, using a pair of mechanical arms that SpaceX calls the "chopsticks." The booster must slow from about 1 km/s to a near-hover in roughly 40 seconds using only its 13 center Raptor engines, then slide into the catch arms with a positional accuracy of about 30 centimeters. If the catch fails, the booster falls back to a softer-than-normal splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico, but a single failed catch would still destroy the launch tower infrastructure.

**IFT-3: First to Space (March 14, 2024)**

The third integrated flight test of Starship lifted off from Starbase at 8:25 AM Central time on March 14, 2024. The mission's primary objective was to demonstrate that Starship could reach orbital insertion velocity and perform a controlled re-entry over the Indian Ocean. The Super Heavy booster successfully separated from the upper stage about 2.5 minutes after liftoff, after which the booster conducted a controlled flip and boostback burn. Although the booster's landing burn was terminated early and it broke apart in the upper atmosphere rather than completing a soft splashdown, the upper stage continued on its planned trajectory.

The Starship upper stage reached a peak altitude of 234 kilometers, surpassing the Kármán line (100 km) and the previous flight record for the vehicle, and achieved a velocity of approximately 26,700 km/h, enough for orbital insertion. The stage performed a planned propellant transfer demonstration between two internal tanks, an important milestone for long-duration missions. About 40 minutes after liftoff, the Starship began its controlled re-entry over the Indian Ocean, but lost contact with the ground before completing the planned soft splashdown. Despite the loss, SpaceX considered the flight a success: every primary objective was met.

**IFT-4: First Controlled Re-Entry (June 6, 2024)**

The fourth integrated flight test launched at 7:50 AM Central on June 6, 2024. IFT-4 repeated the IFT-3 trajectory with several upgrades: improved heatshield tiles, a more reliable propulsion system, and an upgraded propellant transfer demonstration. The Super Heavy booster again completed boostback and a sub-orbital splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico. The Starship upper stage achieved a peak altitude of about 210 kilometers and again reached orbital insertion velocity.

The mission's defining moment came during the re-entry. Starship hit the upper atmosphere at approximately 27,000 km/h, experiencing peak heating of about 1,500 °C on the windward side of the vehicle. The heatshield, made of approximately 18,000 hexagonal silica-tile panels, kept the vehicle's interior within operating temperature throughout the re-entry. Despite plasma-stripping damage to the windward tiles and the loss of around 15 percent of the tile coverage during peak heating, the vehicle maintained attitude control and continued transmitting telemetry all the way to a soft vertical splashdown in the Indian Ocean. This was the first time any spacecraft of this size had survived a controlled atmospheric re-entry from orbital velocity.

**IFT-5: First Booster Catch (October 13, 2024)**

The fifth integrated flight test, launched at 6:25 AM Central on October 13, 2024, was the first to attempt the booster catch. IFT-5 used a slightly different trajectory from IFT-3 and IFT-4, with the Super Heavy booster targeting a return-to-launch-site profile. After a clean separation, the booster executed a boostback burn and returned to Starbase approximately 6.5 minutes after launch. The booster's 13 center Raptor engines fired in a synchronized hover-and-slide maneuver, with the launch tower's chopstick arms closing around the booster at a reported vertical speed of less than 1 m/s at the moment of contact. The catch was successful, the first time any orbital-class booster had been caught by its own launch tower. The Starship upper stage also completed a soft splashdown in the Indian Ocean about 1 hour 6 minutes after launch.

**IFT-6: Booster Caught, Ship Lost (November 19, 2024)**

The sixth integrated flight test, launched at 3:00 PM Central on November 19, 2024, repeated the booster-catch attempt. The Super Heavy booster was successfully caught by the launch tower's chopsticks, the second such catch in the test campaign. However, the Starship upper stage experienced an anomaly during its sub-orbital coast phase. The ship's engines fired briefly to perform a planned demo, but the vehicle's flight computer subsequently detected a fault, and contact was lost before the planned soft splashdown in the Indian Ocean. SpaceX attributed the loss to a propellant leak that developed during the coast phase; the investigation is ongoing as of early 2025.

**IFT-7: Catching Both Stages (January 16, 2025)**

The seventh integrated flight test, launched at 4:38 PM Central on January 16, 2025, achieved a long-planned goal: catching both the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage on the same launch tower. After booster separation, the Super Heavy returned to Starbase and was caught by the chopsticks for the third time. The Starship upper stage continued on a sub-orbital trajectory and, after about 37 minutes of coast, re-entered the atmosphere and was successfully caught by the chopsticks mounted on a second launch tower at Starbase. This was the first time any orbital-class upper stage had been caught by a ground structure, and the first time any launch system had demonstrated rapid reuse of both stages within a single mission.

**The Broader Significance**

The 2024–2025 Starship test campaign demonstrated several firsts that are reshaping commercial spaceflight. The full reuse of both stages of an orbital launch system is the long-sought goal of the launch industry, and Starship is the first vehicle to have demonstrated it (if only for a single test flight). The 33-engine booster is the most powerful rocket stage ever flown. The 121-meter fully-stacked vehicle is the tallest ever flown. The chopstick catch of a 200-ton booster at the launch tower is the most ambitious mechanical recovery operation ever attempted. Every other launch provider — including NASA's Space Launch System, ULA's Vulcan, Blue Origin's New Glenn, Rocket Lab's Neutron, and Europe's Ariane 6 — is now revising its long-term architecture to compete with or partner with SpaceX's fully-reusable approach. The 2024–2025 test campaign is being studied as the textbook example of the iterative design philosophy that SpaceX founder Elon Musk has championed since the company's founding: build, fly, fail, fix, fly again, repeat.
